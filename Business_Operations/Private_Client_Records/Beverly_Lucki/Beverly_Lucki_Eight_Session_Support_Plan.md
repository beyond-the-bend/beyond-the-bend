# Beverly Lucki

## Eight Session Somatic and Restorative Support Plan

## Purpose

This is a flexible support framework for Laura's private work with Beverly. It is not a treatment protocol. The plan should respond to Beverly's condition each week and remain within Laura's scope of somatic movement, restorative support, embodied awareness, relaxation, and compassionate presence.

Beverly's therapist is responsible for EMDR, grief therapy, trauma processing, diagnosis, and mental health treatment. Laura's role is to support stabilization, embodiment, rest, choice, and integration.

## Established Foundation

Beverly has attended Laura's classes for more than a year. Settling into an emotion, noticing physical sensation, and remaining present with a feeling are established parts of their shared work. Laura knows Beverly and has observed her responses to these practices over time.

The invitation to settle into sadness was therefore not a new or unsupported exercise. It was a familiar practice applied to Beverly's present grief. Beverly understood the invitation and experienced it as relieving.

## Central Intentions

1. Help Beverly experience the present moment as sufficiently safe and supported.
2. Rebuild connection with her body gradually.
3. Help her notice grief without being flooded by it.
4. Reduce entanglement with racing thoughts without demanding mental silence.
5. Strengthen her ability to move between grief and restorative contact with life.
6. Build a small collection of practices she can use independently.
7. Coordinate carefully with her therapist if Beverly provides written consent.
8. Continue direct and compassionate safety check ins.

## A Consistent Session Structure

Predictability may be more valuable than novelty. Most sessions can follow this general rhythm:

1. Arrival and safety check, 10 minutes.

Ask how Beverly has been since the previous meeting, how her lung symptoms are, whether thoughts about death have changed, and what she needs today. Ask about her capacity rather than assuming it.

2. Orientation and support, 10 minutes.

Keep the eyes open at first. Notice the room, colours, light, sounds, the chair, the floor, and places where her body is supported. Invite rather than direct.

3. Gentle movement, 10 to 15 minutes.

Use small, predictable movements. Examples include feet pressing gently into the floor, slow ankle or wrist movements, supported shoulder movement, a small seated twist, or a comfortable rocking motion. Avoid intense breathwork or movements that aggravate her lungs.

4. Restorative or somatic practice, 15 minutes.

Choose one primary practice rather than many. Move slowly and check whether inward attention is settling or intensifying distress.

5. Integration and return, 10 minutes.

Reorient to the room. Ask what she notices now, what helped, what did not help, and what she wants to carry into the week.

6. One small home practice, 5 minutes.

Offer one practice that takes one to three minutes. More is not necessarily better.

## Helping With the Racing Mind

Do not make quieting the mind the test of success. Trying to stop thoughts can create another struggle. Help Beverly shift from being inside the storm to noticing that the storm is present.

Useful options include:

1. Name three things she sees, two things she hears, and one place where her body is supported.
2. Place both feet on the floor and alternate gentle pressure between them.
3. Say, "My mind is trying to solve everything right now."
4. Write urgent thoughts on paper and tell the mind they have been recorded.
5. Choose one neutral sensation, such as the hands touching, rather than scanning the entire body.
6. Use a slightly longer exhale only if it feels natural and does not strain her breathing.
7. Shift between ten seconds of sadness and twenty or thirty seconds of noticing the room or physical support.

The goal is flexible attention, not an empty mind.

## Helping Her Be With Grief

Laura can trust the embodied language and practices that she and Beverly have already established. She can invite Beverly to settle into sadness without immediately limiting the experience to a predetermined number of seconds. The appropriate duration should emerge from Beverly's present response, her ability to remain connected with her body, and Laura's knowledge of her.

Laura can ask:

"Would it feel manageable to notice a little of the sadness right now?"

"Where do you notice it most clearly?"

"Can you also feel the chair or the floor while that sadness is here?"

"Would you like to stay with this, step back, move, or look around the room?"

This allows grief and present support to exist together. If Beverly becomes more agitated, absent, numb, confused, breathless, or unable to orient, reduce inward focus and return to the room, movement, water, and choice.

The purpose of returning to external support is not to interrupt genuine sadness prematurely. It is an available option if Beverly moves beyond feeling the sadness into becoming overwhelmed or disconnected.

## What Acceptance Means

Acceptance is not approval, peace with injustice, or the disappearance of grief. It is the gradual ability to recognize, "This pain is here because these losses are real," without adding the demand that she should not feel it or must solve it immediately.

Laura does not need to persuade Beverly to be okay with grief. The more useful experience may be, "I am not okay with what happened, and I can be accompanied and supported in this moment."

## What Letting Go Means

Avoid asking Beverly to let go of grief or of the people she loves. Invite her to experiment with releasing:

1. The need to explain every feeling.
2. The pressure to fix grief.
3. The belief that rest is avoidance.
4. The demand to protect everyone else at every moment.
5. Muscular holding that is ready to soften.
6. One thought or task that does not need attention right now.

Letting go may be temporary and very small. It may mean putting something down for thirty seconds and discovering that she can return to it later.

## Session One

### Theme

Safety, orientation, and being supported.

### Intention

Help Beverly leave with a direct experience of her body being held by the room, the floor, and the props.

### Possible Practices

1. Open eyed orientation.
2. Noticing feet and seat.
3. Small seated or lying movements.
4. A fully supported restorative position that does not restrict breathing.
5. Gentle hand contact chosen by Beverly, such as one hand resting on the thigh or upper chest.

### Home Practice

Notice one point of physical support for thirty seconds, three times each day.

## Session Two

### Theme

Contact and return.

### Intention

Introduce the idea that she can touch sadness and then return to present support.

### Possible Practices

1. Orientation.
2. Alternating attention between sadness and a neutral sensation.
3. Gentle pressing through the feet or hands.
4. Rest with eyes open or softly focused.

### Important Context

Beverly begins with her EMDR therapist on July 2, 2026. Ask whether both appointments occur on the same day. If so, keep this session especially simple and avoid emotionally evocative work.

### Home Practice

Settle into the physical experience of sadness without judging, analyzing, explaining, or trying to fix it. Remain for as long as it feels honest and manageable. Remember that opening the eyes, moving, pausing, or reconnecting with the surroundings are always available.

## Session Three

### Theme

Boundaries and containment.

### Intention

Help Beverly sense that feelings can exist within a body that also has edges, support, and choice.

### Possible Practices

1. Feeling the outline of the body against a chair or blanket.
2. Gentle self contact with explicit consent.
3. Pressing hands into a wall or cushion.
4. Supported child's pose only if comfortable and emotionally neutral for her.
5. A clear ending ritual that marks the return to daily life.

### Home Practice

Choose one phrase such as, "This is here, and I do not have to solve it right now."

## Session Four

### Theme

Anger as information and energy.

### Intention

Allow safe recognition of anger without analyzing friendships or encouraging discharge.

### Possible Practices

1. Feet pressing into the floor.
2. Hands pressing into a wall.
3. Slow pushing and releasing against a cushion.
4. Noticing where strength and boundaries are present.
5. Returning to rest after activation.

### Home Practice

When anger appears, ask, "What is this anger trying to protect?" She does not need to answer immediately.

## Session Five

### Theme

Rest without abandonment.

### Intention

Explore whether Beverly can rest briefly without feeling that she is avoiding grief or abandoning the people she loves.

### Possible Practices

1. Supported reclining or side lying.
2. Warmth, weight, and blankets if desired.
3. Quiet music or nature sounds.
4. Gentle attention to flowers or another life giving image.
5. Brief periods of silence with regular check ins.

### Home Practice

Choose one five minute act of rest and name it as care rather than avoidance.

## Session Six

### Theme

Connection and continuing bonds.

### Intention

Support the possibility that love and grief can coexist and that connection to her children does not depend upon remaining in constant pain.

### Possible Practices

1. Grounding through hands and heart only if comfortable.
2. A meaningful object or memory chosen by Beverly.
3. Noticing where love is felt physically without forcing positive emotion.
4. Movement that opens and closes gently, emphasizing choice.

### Home Practice

Spend a brief, chosen period with something that connects her to love, then deliberately return to an ordinary activity.

## Session Seven

### Theme

Restoration and small signs of life.

### Intention

Notice what is still alive or meaningful without asking Beverly to feel joyful.

### Possible Practices

1. Time with flowers or nature if practical.
2. Gentle standing or walking awareness.
3. Movement guided by preference rather than correction.
4. Identifying what gives one percent more ease or interest.

### Home Practice

Notice one moment of interest, beauty, comfort, or connection. No joy is required.

## Session Eight

### Theme

Integration and continuation.

### Intention

Review what Beverly has learned about her body, grief, mental activity, safety, and support. Decide what should continue.

### Possible Practices

1. Beverly chooses the practices that have helped most.
2. Review her personal signs of overwhelm.
3. Create a short sequence for difficult days.
4. Identify professional and personal supports.
5. Discuss whether further sessions are appropriate and how they complement therapy.

### Home Practice

Use her personalized sequence and continue with therapeutic support.

## Safety and Referral Guide

At each meeting, ask briefly whether Beverly's thoughts about death have changed. A calm question does not create suicidal thoughts.

If she reports active intent, a plan, access to a method, inability to remain safe, or imminent danger, do not continue with a routine somatic session. Stay with her, involve emergency or crisis support, and follow professional guidance. In Canada, call or text 988. If immediate safety is at risk, call 911.

If sadness, withdrawal, self care, sleep, eating, confusion, lung symptoms, or daily functioning worsen, encourage prompt contact with her therapist and physician.

## Signs to Slow Down

1. She becomes less oriented to the room.
2. She feels unreal, far away, numb, or absent.
3. Her breathing becomes strained.
4. She cannot respond to simple choices.
5. Distress continues rising rather than settling.
6. She feels pressured to produce emotion.
7. She leaves sessions more destabilized for an extended period.

When these signs appear, use less inward attention, more orientation, smaller movements, open eyes, practical support, and collaboration with her therapist.
