## Personal Intro

Hello,

This morning I came across a Mother's Day message from Sarah McLachlan, and something in it cracked me open just a little.

She has been a voice in my life since long before I became a mother. Her music found me in my younger years and stayed, the way certain art does when it touches something true in you. So when she spoke about Mother's Day the way it actually feels for so many of us, not just the flowers and the warmth and the beautiful memories, but the grief and the longing and the moments we wish we could reach back and do differently, I felt it land somewhere deep.

I sat with that for a while.

Because I think there is a version of Mother's Day that we do not talk about very much. It lives alongside the gratitude and the love, quietly, in the same body, on the same day. It is the version where you look back across the years and feel the beauty and the ache all at once, not separately, but woven together in a way that is hard to explain to someone who has not felt it.

### The Weight We Carry Quietly

*By Laura Harvey | Beyond the Bend*

I have two adult children. And when I think about those years of raising them, what rises in me is not one clean feeling. It is everything at the same time. The moments that still make my heart full. And the moments I am still learning to forgive myself for.

I was never not trying. Not for a single day. Every decision I made, I made because I believed with my whole heart that it was right. I pushed as hard as I possibly could to show up the way I wanted to show up. And still, there are places where I know I fell short. Places where my own brokenness got in the way of the mother I so desperately wanted to be.

That is a particular kind of grief. It is not failure exactly. It is something quieter and harder to name, the grief of loving more than you knew how to hold, inside a season of your own life when you were still being shaped yourself. When you were still becoming.

I wonder how many women sat with that today, quietly, underneath the celebrations. Because I did.

And perhaps what I am finding my way toward, slowly and not always gracefully, is that there comes a point where we have to make a choice. Not to pretend the past was different than it was. Not to minimize the impact of our mistakes on the people we love most. But to finally, tenderly, extend to ourselves the same understanding we would offer any other human being who loved that hard and was that broken at the same time.

We were human. We are human. And human is messy and imperfect and does not follow the script we wrote for ourselves before we knew how hard it would actually be.

Mothering does not end when our children become adults. I believe that more deeply now than I ever have. And perhaps the gifts I was not able to give my children then, because I was still in the middle of becoming myself, are the ones I am learning to offer now. A steadier presence. A softer heart. The hard won wisdom that only comes from having walked through the fire and chosen, on the other side, to stop punishing yourself for having burned.

That path runs through forgiveness. Not the kind that dismisses what happened, but the kind that says I did the best I could with who I was, and who I am now is still growing, still softening, still learning to be gentle with myself so that I can be genuinely present for the people I love.

That is the work. Not just the yoga or the breathwork or the stillness, though those are part of it. It is the deeper practice of coming home to yourself, again and again, with a little less judgment each time. Of learning to hold your own heart the way you always wished someone had held it for you.

And if our children end up in therapy somewhere along the way, well, perhaps that is just part of the cycle of becoming. We do our best. They do their own becoming. And with any grace at all, we find our way back to each other, changed and more honest and perhaps more capable of real love than we ever were before.

## Practice of the Week

### A Letter to the Woman in the Photo

This week, I invite you to find an old photograph of yourself from a time when your children were young, or from a season of your life that felt particularly heavy.

Instead of looking at it with a critical eye, try to look at the woman in the picture with deep, genuine compassion. Silently acknowledge how hard she was trying, how much she loved, and how she continued to show up even when she was exhausted.

Then, take a piece of paper and write a short letter to her. Tell her exactly what she desperately needed to hear back then. Offer her the grace, understanding, and forgiveness that she may not have known how to give herself. You do not have to fix her mistakes. Just let her know that she was, and always has been, enough.
