### New ideas

- Explore the word "afferent" as part of Laura's somatic vocabulary. Afferent signals are the signals travelling upward from the body toward the brain. This connects beautifully with somatic work, gut feeling, nervous system listening, and the vagus nerve. This could become newsletter language, teaching language in class, or part of a deeper educational piece about why the body is not just receiving instructions from the brain. The body is constantly speaking back.

- Explore "bottom up" somatic theory. In the nervous system, especially in relation to the vagus nerve, bottom up refers to information travelling from the body toward the brain rather than the brain simply directing the body from the top down. This language could help explain why somatic work is so powerful. We are not only changing thoughts. We are listening to the body and allowing bodily signals to inform the brain, the nervous system, and our sense of self. Add lineage note: the terminology comes from cognitive psychology and perception research. James Gibson, an American psychologist, described bottom up processing in the 1960s as a way of understanding how we perceive the world from sensory information upward. Peter Levine later applied a similar framework to trauma and the nervous system. Levine did not originate the term itself, but he helped bring this body first understanding into trauma healing and somatic work.

- Plain language explanation of bottom up work: bottom up means starting with the body and nervous system first, rather than trying to think your way through things or talk your way out of distress. In somatic work, we recognize that trauma, stress, and dysregulation are held not only in thoughts, but also in sensation, breath, posture, bracing, movement impulses, gut responses, and physical survival patterns. When we work gently with what is happening in the body, the mind and emotions often begin to shift because the nervous system is receiving new information. Top down work moves in the other direction, using thought, logic, interpretation, and conversation to create change. Peter Levine's work emphasizes that the nervous system often needs to complete or reorganize survival responses at the body level before the mind can truly settle.

- Develop a future three hour vagus nerve workshop. Possible focus: what the vagus nerve is, why it matters, how it relates to the body, breath, stress, safety, gut feeling, and emotional regulation. The workshop could combine clear teaching with physical practice, breathwork, mindfulness, somatic inquiry, and simple embodied experiments so students can begin to feel and understand their own nervous system from the inside.

- Develop future work around the psoas muscle. Laura has Liz Koch's book, The Psoas Book, and wants to study the psoas more deeply. Possible directions include a workshop, an online course, or a series of classes exploring the psoas as a powerful muscle connected to the somatic system, stress, instinct, posture, breath, movement, and deep inner holding.

## Newsletter Ideas

Themes, stories, subject lines, reflections, practices, and weekly newsletter sparks.

- Write a newsletter or series of newsletters about feminine archetypes and goddesses who can teach us something about ourselves. This could include Persephone, who is taken into the underworld differently than Inanna, but is transformed by that descent and becomes Queen of the Underworld, gaining a different kind of power and wisdom. It could include Hecate, goddess of crossroads, thresholds, transformation, and moving between worlds. It could include Lilith, often seen as the first woman who chose her own path, and Ariadne, who helps Theseus, is abandoned, and then has to find her own sovereignty. This theme could speak deeply to midlife women who are crossing thresholds, reclaiming power, and discovering that transformation is not always chosen in a simple way, but can still become a source of wisdom.

- Write a newsletter around Chiron and the wounded healer archetype, possibly following the recent Inanna descent newsletter. Chiron, from Greek mythology, is the immortal centaur who carried an incurable wound but became a great healer, teacher, and guide for others. The newsletter could explore how some wounds do not simply disappear, but become part of the way we learn to see, listen, and accompany others. This could connect deeply with women in midlife, especially the way lived experience, struggle, grief, exhaustion, body changes, family history, burnout, or old pain can become a source of compassion and wisdom rather than proof that we are broken. Laura could write from inside her own recognition that many of the wounds, questions, and difficult seasons she has carried are now becoming part of how she supports other women. The tone should be humble and reflective, not "I am healed and therefore I heal others," but more like, "I am beginning to understand that the places I have had to tend in myself may also be the places from which I can offer something real."

- Write about the word "afferent" and the idea that the body is always sending information upward to the brain. This could connect to gut feelings, body wisdom, the vagus nerve, and Laura's recurring theme that healing is not only about changing thoughts. It is also about changing where and how we listen.

- Write about "bottom up" healing or bottom up listening. This could become a newsletter about why somatic work begins with sensation, breath, weight, orientation, and subtle body signals rather than starting only with the thinking mind. If using the term historically, note that bottom up processing came from cognitive psychology and perception research, especially James Gibson's work in the 1960s, before later being applied to trauma and nervous system work by teachers such as Peter Levine.

- Possible newsletter language for bottom up work: Instead of asking, "How do I think my way out of this?" we might begin by asking, "What is my body doing right now? Where am I bracing? What is my breath doing? What does my nervous system think it needs to protect me from?" This could help explain why somatic work is not about ignoring the mind, but about beginning at the level where the body is already speaking.

- Possible theme: "The body is not just something the mind manages." This could introduce afferent signals in plain language and invite readers to notice what information their body may already be offering before the mind rushes in to interpret.
