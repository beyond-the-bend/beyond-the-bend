*June 22, 2026*

## Personal Intro

Hello,

Some mornings I wake up and my body simply doesn't want to move. It isn't always because I slept badly, and it doesn't necessarily mean that anything is wrong. Sometimes there is just a heaviness there, a quiet reluctance to begin, and lately I have been wondering whether my first response needs to be fixing it.

We live in a culture that treats stillness like a problem to solve. If we're not feeling motivated or ready to take on the day, it can seem as though something must be wrong. But I'm beginning to notice that sometimes the heaviness, the numbness, or the desire to stay under the covers a little longer isn't something to fight. Sometimes it's simply the body asking us to slow down long enough to notice what it has been carrying.

I wanted to share a little of that inquiry with you this week, because I suspect many of us have learned to override ourselves long before we have learned how to listen.

### Some Mornings the Body Just Doesn't Want to Move

*By Laura Harvey | Beyond the Bend Yoga*

Over the years, I've come to believe that some of the most important practices we have are not the ones that look impressive from the outside. They're the moments when we stop trying to fix ourselves and become willing to simply notice what is here.

One of the things I often do when I feel heavy or disconnected is place one hand on my chest and one hand on my belly. Not because it's magical, and not because it makes everything better, but because it helps me pause long enough to listen. I don't ask myself how to get rid of the feeling. I don't immediately reach for the right book, the right podcast, or the right mindset. I just get curious.

Where is this sitting in my body? What does it actually feel like? Is it tight, heavy, hollow, or restless? Can I stay with it for thirty seconds without making it mean that I'm failing or that something is wrong?

I have been wondering how often the body speaks quietly before it has to speak loudly. A little fatigue, a clenched jaw, a shallow breath, or the wish to withdraw may not be an inconvenience standing between us and the day we planned. It may be information. It may be the body's way of saying that something has been carried for long enough and needs our attention.

Listening doesn't mean that we abandon every responsibility whenever we feel tired. Sometimes we still get up, make breakfast, go to work, care for the people we love, and do what the day requires. But there is a difference between moving forward while staying connected to ourselves and dragging the body along as though it were an obstacle. One is a relationship. The other is an act of abandonment.

I'm not always good at knowing the difference. I still have mornings when I reach for productivity before presence, when I decide what I should be feeling before I have even noticed what I am feeling. But I am learning that the pause itself changes something. Even when nothing is solved, being met with curiosity feels different from being met with judgment.

Perhaps this is the deeper heart of practice. The poses, the breathwork, and the routines can be beautiful supports, but they are not the whole of it. The heart of the practice is honesty. It is the willingness to acknowledge where we are and meet ourselves there with kindness.

Maybe that is what the body is asking for when it slows us down. Not for us to become better, but simply to become present. There is wisdom in that kind of listening, even when what we hear isn't what we were hoping for.

## Practice of the Week

### A Thirty Second Listening Practice

This week, if you notice yourself feeling heavy, unmotivated, or disconnected, place one hand on your heart and one hand on your belly. Take three slow breaths without trying to make them deeper or better than they naturally are.

Then ask yourself, *What am I carrying right now?*

Notice what appears as sensation before you begin searching for an explanation. Is there tightness, warmth, pressure, restlessness, emptiness, or something you cannot yet name? Let thirty seconds be enough.

Then ask, *What would feel supportive in this moment?* Not what should I do, and not what needs to get done, but what would genuinely offer support. It might be movement, rest, a glass of water, a conversation, a yoga class, or simply permission to stop arguing with how you feel.

You do not have to solve what you discover. The practice is simply to let the body know that someone is listening.
